Part B: Solids, Liquids, and Gases

The same substance can be a solid, a liquid, or a gas. What changes is how the particles are arranged and how they move.

WordWhat it meansTry it
particleA particle is a tiny piece of matter, like an atom or a molecule. It is a general word for the tiny pieces that make up a substance.The ice cube is made of tiny ______ that we cannot see.
solidIn a solid, particles are packed closely together in a fixed pattern. They vibrate but do not move from place to place.Ice, a rock, and a desk are all ______.
liquidIn a liquid, particles are close together but can slide past each other and move around.Water, juice, and oil are all ______.
gasIn a gas, particles are spread far apart and move quickly in every direction.Steam, air, and helium in a balloon are all ______.
particle spacingParticle spacing describes how close or far apart particles are from each other.In a solid, the particle spacing is ______ (close / far apart).
particle motionParticle motion describes how the particles move: vibrating in place, sliding past each other, or moving freely.In a gas, the particle motion is ______ (fast and free / slow and fixed).
kinetic energyKinetic energy is the energy of motion. Particles with more kinetic energy move faster.Gas particles have ______ (more / less) kinetic energy than solid particles.

Part C: Changes of State

A change of state happens when thermal energy is added or removed. Adding energy makes particles move faster and spread apart. Removing energy makes them slow down and move closer together.

energyEnergy is the ability to cause change or do work. This unit focuses on thermal energy and kinetic energy.It takes ______ to melt an ice cube.
temperatureTemperature measures how much kinetic energy the particles in a substance have, on average. We measure it in °C.A higher ______ means the particles are moving faster, on average.
thermal energyThermal energy is the total energy of all the moving particles in a substance.Adding ______ makes particles move faster and spread farther apart.

Word parts

inter- means "between." Intermolecular forces are the forces between molecules, not the forces holding one molecule together.

Reading a Heating Curve

This is the graph that confuses people, so take your time with it. The line goes up, then goes flat, then up again, then flat again.

Why do the flat parts happen?

This is the big idea of the whole unit, and it is worth saying carefully.

The key idea

During a flat part, you are still adding thermal energy. The temperature does not go up because that energy is being used to break the attractions between particles instead of making the particles move faster.

Temperature only rises when particles speed up. While a substance is melting or boiling, the energy is doing a different job, so the thermometer does not change.

  • Going up: particles are speeding up, so temperature rises.
  • Flat: particles are pulling apart from each other, so temperature stays the same.
  • A cooling curve is the same graph in reverse. The line goes down, then flat while the substance freezes or condenses, then down again.

When you write a CER

Your claim answers the question in one sentence. Your evidence is the data or observation you actually saw. Your reasoning explains the evidence using particles and energy. Most marks are lost in the reasoning, so always name what the particles are doing.

Check Yourself

Answer each one before you click. Say your answer out loud first.

1. A puddle on the playground disappears by the end of the day. What change of state happened, and where did the energy come from?

Evaporation. The liquid water slowly changed into a gas at its surface. The thermal energy came from the Sun and the warm air, which gave the water particles enough kinetic energy to escape into the air.

2. You heat a beaker of ice. For two minutes the thermometer stays at 0°C even though the flame is still on. Is the energy disappearing? Explain.

No. The energy is still going in. It is being used to break the attractions holding the ice particles in their fixed pattern, which is melting. Because the particles are not speeding up yet, the temperature does not change. Once all the ice has melted, the temperature starts to rise again.

3. Water droplets appear on the outside of a cold glass. Where did the water come from, and what is this change called?

The water came from water vapour already in the air, not from inside the glass. The cold glass removed thermal energy from the gas particles near it, they slowed down, and the attractions pulled them together into a liquid. This is condensation.

4. A gas fills the whole room, but a solid keeps its shape. Explain the difference using particle spacing and particle motion.

In a gas, the particles are far apart with weak attractions and they move fast in every direction, so they spread out until they fill the container. In a solid, the particles are packed closely in a fixed pattern with strong attractions, so they only vibrate in place and the shape stays the same.

5. Dry ice turns straight into fog without making a puddle. What is this change called, and what did it skip?

Sublimation. It skipped the liquid state, changing directly from a solid to a gas.

6. Two objects are the same size, but one feels much heavier. Which property is different, and how would you calculate it?

Density. They have the same volume but different mass. Density = mass ÷ volume, so the heavier object has the higher density.
